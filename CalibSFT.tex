\section{Introduction}

Reasoning large language models (LLMs) increasingly solve complex problems by generating a reasoning trajectory followed by the answer~\citep{yang2025qwen3}. 
Despite these advances, the answer accuracy alone does not ensure reliable uncertainty estimates: a model may produce plausible reasoning trajectory yet remain overconfident in an incorrect answer~\citep{geng2024survey, liu2025uncertainty}. 
In principle, confidence calibration requires the reported confidence to reflect the empirical probability that the answer is correct, which in turn enables applications such as hallucination detection \citep{wu2026mitigating} and adaptive test-time scaling \citep{yang2026scaling}.

Verbalized confidence obtains such an answer-level score by asking the model to emit a numerical probability with its final answer. Unlike confidence estimators based on token probabilities, hidden states, or repeated sampling~\citep{luo2026your,srey2026signals,wang2026calibrating}, it can be produced in a single black-box generation~\citep{xiong2024can}. 

\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Verbalized Confidence and Confidence-Aware RL}

In this work, we study the calibration of verbalized numerical confidence in reasoning language models~\citep{tian2023just,xiong2024can}. Given a question $x$, the LLM
$\pi_\theta$ generates a response $o=(r,a,c)$, where $r$ is a reasoning
trace, $a$ is an answer, and $c\in[0,1]$ is a post-answer confidence
value that represents the model's estimated probability that $a$ is
correct. A task verifier assigns the response a binary correctness
label $z=V(x,a)\in\{0,1\}$.

\paragraph{Confidence-aware rewards.}
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains the policy
with the correctness label $z$ as the reward. To further incorporate
confidence into the optimization objective, confidence-aware RL adds a
calibration reward that depends on both $c$ and $z$. For example,
RLCR~\citep{damani2026beyond} sets the calibration reward to the
negative Brier loss. The resulting total reward favors correct
answers and, for a given correctness, prefers confidence closer to $z$:
\begin{equation}
    R(o)=z-(c-z)^2.
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Policy optimization.}
Confidence-aware RL typically optimizes this reward with Group Relative
Policy Optimization (GRPO)~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. For each
question, GRPO samples $G$ responses $\{o_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from the old
policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ and normalizes their rewards
$R_i=R(o_i)$ within the group to obtain the advantage
\begin{equation}
    \widehat A_i=\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\sigma_R+\epsilon},\qquad
    \bar R=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_j,\qquad
    \sigma_R=\operatorname{std}\!\left(\{R_j\}_{j=1}^{G}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon>0$ ensures numerical stability. GRPO then updates the
policy by maximizing
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{L_i}\sum_{t=1}^{L_i}\min\Big(\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}},
1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\right)\widehat A_i\Big)\Bigg],
\end{equation}
where $o_{i,t}$ is the $t$-th token of $o_i$, $L_i$ is the length of
$o_i$, $\rho_{i,t}(\theta)=\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid x,o_{i,<t})/
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid x,o_{i,<t})$ is the importance
ratio, and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}$ is the clipping parameter.

\subsection{Calibration Metrics}

A useful verbalized confidence score should be both calibrated and
discriminative~\citep{eusebi2026reading}. Let $C$ and $Z$ denote the
confidence and correctness random variables. Confidence is perfectly
calibrated if $\Pr(Z=1\mid C=p)=p$ for every $p\in[0,1]$, and it is
discriminative if correct responses receive higher confidence than
incorrect ones.

\textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE)}~\citep{guo2017calibration}
measures the deviation from perfect calibration,
$\mathbb E_C[|\Pr(Z=1\mid C)-C|]$. After partitioning $N$ responses into
$B$ confidence bins $\{\mathcal I_b\}_{b=1}^{B}$, we estimate it as
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{ECE}
    =\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|\mathcal I_b|}{N}
    \left|\operatorname{acc}(\mathcal I_b)-
    \operatorname{conf}(\mathcal I_b)\right|,
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{acc}(\mathcal I_b)$ and
$\operatorname{conf}(\mathcal I_b)$ are the mean correctness and
confidence in bin $b$.
While ECE compares confidence with accuracy at the bin level, the
\textbf{Brier score}~\citep{brier1950verification},
$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(c_j-z_j)^2$, measures the squared error
between confidence and correctness for each response and combines
calibration and resolution~\citep{murphy1973new}.
However, calibration alone does not imply discrimination. For example,
assigning every response a constant confidence equal to the overall
accuracy yields zero ECE while giving correct and incorrect responses
identical scores~\citep{eusebi2026reading}. We therefore also report
\textbf{AUROC}, a threshold-independent measure of how well confidence
discriminates between correct and incorrect responses.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Confidence-aware RL typically adds a calibration reward that scores each response's
confidence against its
correctness~\citep{xu2024sayself,damani2026beyond,ma2026decoupling,yang2026scaling}.
Whether this reward alone produces well-calibrated confidence depends on
the distribution of sampled confidence values. We therefore examine this
distribution before RL, during training, and at test time.

\paragraph{Confidence priors concentrate on a few high values.}
We refer to the confidence distribution before RL as the model's
\emph{confidence prior}.
To characterize this prior, we sample 10 responses per question from
Qwen3-8B~\citep{yang2025qwen3}, Gemma4-E2B-Instruct~\citep{team2026gemma},
and OLMo3-7B-Instruct~\citep{olmo2025olmo3} on DeepScaleR-Train.
Figure~\ref{fig:initial_confidence_prior} presents their confidence
distributions for correct and incorrect responses.
All three models place most of their confidence mass on a few high values, with fewer than 5\% of outputs below 0.5.
Confidence remains high even for incorrect responses, so it does not
track answer correctness.
RL therefore starts from a concentrated confidence prior that rarely
produces low-confidence samples.

\paragraph{RL improves calibration but confidence concentration persists.}
To examine whether confidence-aware RL overcomes this concentration,
we train Qwen3-8B with RLCR~\citep{damani2026beyond}.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rlcr_training_calibration}(a), RLCR reduces
Brier loss over training, showing that the calibration reward can be
optimized.
However, Figure~\ref{fig:rlcr_training_calibration}(b) shows that
the model continues to sample a small set of confidence values per
question, and confidence token entropy stays low throughout training
(see Appendix~\ref{sec:motivation_training_statistics}
for training curves across RL objectives).

To better understand why this concentration persists during training,
we formulate confidence generation as action selection under a
softmax policy~\citep{garg2022alternate}. Since the LLM decodes each
confidence value autoregressively, we model the induced distribution
over the finite set of confidence values as a softmax over per-value
logits.
\begin{proposition}[Policy gradients under confidence concentration]
\label{prop:confidence_saturation}
Given a question $x$ and a finite set of confidence actions
$\{c_1,\ldots,c_K\}\subseteq[0,1]$,
let $\eta=(\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_K)$ denote the policy's logits for these
actions and $\pi_i=\Pr(C=c_i\mid x)=\exp(\eta_i)/\sum_{j=1}^K\exp(\eta_j)$.
Define the fixed expected reward $u_i=-\mathbb E[(c_i-Z)^2\mid x]$.
Let $J_x(\eta)=\sum_i\pi_i u_i$ and
$\Delta_x=\max_i u_i-\min_i u_i$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial J_x}{\partial\eta_i}=\pi_i(u_i-J_x),
    \qquad
    \left|\frac{\partial J_x}{\partial\eta_i}\right|
    \leq\pi_i\Delta_x.
\end{equation}
If $\pi_k=1-\varepsilon$ for some $k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$, then
\begin{equation}
    \|\nabla_\eta J_x\|_1\leq 2\varepsilon\Delta_x.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_saturation} shows that a rarely sampled
confidence value has a small expected logit gradient even when choosing it
would improve the expected reward. As the confidence distribution concentrates
on a single value, the entire logit gradient vanishes, including when that
value is suboptimal.
A concentrated confidence distribution therefore impedes exploration of
better-calibrated outputs, consistent with the persistently low
distinct-value count and confidence token entropy in
Figure~\ref{fig:rlcr_training_calibration}(b).
We provide the proof in Appendix~\ref{sec:confidence_saturation_proof}.



\paragraph{Persistent confidence concentration hinders downstream calibration.}
We evaluate the final RLCR checkpoint on Math500 and MinervaMath,
two datasets of contrasting difficulty.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rlcr_test_calibration}, the post-RL model
underestimates its accuracy on Math500 (90.5\% Pass@1 vs.\ 84.5\% mean
confidence) but is overconfident on MinervaMath (36.7\% Pass@1
vs.\ 74.8\% mean confidence).
Despite a 53.8-point gap in Pass@1 between the two tasks, mean
confidence differs by only 9.7 points. The three most frequent values
still account for 82.25\% and 69.49\% of outputs on Math500 and
MinervaMath, respectively.
The same small set of confidence values therefore persists across tasks of
different difficulty, so confidence no longer tracks the model's accuracy
on each task.

To characterize the cost of this miscalibration, we bound the expected
Brier loss $\mathbb E[(C-Z)^2\mid x]$, which is minimized at $C=p_x$,
where $p_x=\Pr(Z=1\mid x)$~\citep{gneiting2007strictly,damani2026beyond}.
\begin{proposition}[Brier risk under limited confidence support]
\label{prop:confidence_support}
Assume that $C$ and $Z$ are conditionally independent given $x$,
consistent with the limited discrimination between correct and
incorrect responses observed in Figure~\ref{fig:initial_confidence_prior}.
For any $\delta>0$, let $m_\delta(x)=\Pr(|C-p_x|<\delta\mid x)$.
Then
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[(C-Z)^2\mid x]
    \geq p_x(1-p_x)+\delta^2\bigl(1-m_\delta(x)\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_support} shows that the lower bound on
Brier loss increases as $m_\delta(x)$ decreases, so concentration hurts
calibration whenever the retained values lie far from $p_x$.
In Appendix~\ref{sec:confidence_support_proof}, we provide the detailed
proof, extend the bound to a restricted confidence support, and show
that limited support similarly raises the expected error of the
confidence value closest to $p_x$ among $G$ samples.

\section{Method}

Section~\ref{sec:motivation} showed that confidence concentration
persists during RL, since rarely sampled confidence values have small
logit gradients, and that the resulting narrow support hinders
downstream calibration.
Our key idea is therefore to set the confidence prior by supervision
before RL, rather than leaving it to exploration.
We propose CalibSFT, a supervised fine-tuning stage for confidence
initialization. For each question, CalibSFT samples multiple
responses from the base model and assigns each a confidence
target that combines the question's success rate with the
response's own correctness.
It supervises confidence on all responses, but reasoning and answers
only on correct ones.

\subsection{Proper-Scoring Confidence Targets}

Under a proper scoring rule, the optimal confidence equals the
probability that the answer is correct~\citep{gneiting2007strictly}.
Since this probability is not observed directly, we estimate it from the base
model's own responses. For each training question $x$, we sample
$n$ responses with correctness labels $z_i\in\{0,1\}$ and use
their success rate as the estimate.
However, correct and incorrect responses to the question then share the
same confidence target. To support discrimination, we combine the
success rate with the response-level label $z_i$ and construct the
confidence target of response $i$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:calibsft_target}
    q_x=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_i,
    \qquad
    c^{\star}_i=\lambda q_x+(1-\lambda)z_i,
\end{equation}
where the mixing weight $\lambda\in[0,1]$ balances the two signals.
The mixed target is no longer a correctness label, so we examine
whether it still yields the same optimum under a strictly proper
scoring rule. Let $p_x=\Pr(Z=1\mid x)$ under the base model, and let
$Z_i$ and $C^{\star}_i$ denote the random counterparts of $z_i$ and
$c^{\star}_i$ when the $n$ responses are sampled independently. We then have the
following proposition.
\begin{proposition}[Proper-scoring optimum of the mixed target]
\label{prop:confidence_target_moments}
Let $\ell(a,z)$ be a strictly proper scoring loss for $z\in\{0,1\}$,
extended to $t\in[0,1]$ by $\ell(a,t)=t\,\ell(a,1)+(1-t)\,\ell(a,0)$.
Then $\mathbb E[C^{\star}_i\mid x]=p_x$, and for every $a$ at which
$\ell$ is finite,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[\ell(a,C^{\star}_i)\mid x]=\mathbb E[\ell(a,Z_i)\mid x].
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_target_moments} shows that the mixed
target is equivalent to the correctness label in expected loss, so
$p_x$ remains the optimal prediction under any strictly proper $\ell$.
The number of responses $n$ controls the variance of the success-rate
estimate and the weight $\lambda$ controls the target's discrimination
within a question; we set $n=50$ and $\lambda=0.5$ by default.
We prove the proposition in detail and derive the variance of
$C^{\star}_i$ in Appendix~\ref{sec:confidence_target_proof}.

In practice, we replace each response's confidence with $c^{\star}_i$,
preserving its reasoning and answer.
Since the target values are unevenly distributed across questions,
training on the raw data would again concentrate supervision on a few
target values. We therefore apply target-balanced sampling, which draws
the same number of responses for each target value
(Appendix~\ref{sec:calibsft_data_details}).

\subsection{Correctness-Conditional Supervision}

The constructed data contain both correct and incorrect responses. The
incorrect ones are kept because their lower targets teach the model to
express low confidence in its own errors, whereas imitating their
reasoning would train the model on incorrect solutions.
CalibSFT therefore supervises confidence on all responses and restricts
reasoning and answer supervision to correct ones.

Formally, let $\widetilde o$ denote a response whose confidence has been
replaced by its constructed target, and let $\mathcal T_{\mathrm{ans}}$
and $\mathcal T_{\mathrm{conf}}$ denote the positions of its reasoning
and answer tokens and its confidence tokens, respectively.
The CalibSFT objective is then
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathcal L_{\mathrm{CalibSFT}}={}&(1-\beta)\,\mathbb E_{\mathrm{correct}}
    \left[-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T_{\mathrm{ans}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_{\mathrm{ans}}}
    \log\pi_\theta(\widetilde o_t\mid x,\widetilde o_{<t})\right]\\
    &+\beta\,\mathbb E_{\mathrm{all}}
    \left[-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T_{\mathrm{conf}}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_{\mathrm{conf}}}
    \log\pi_\theta(\widetilde o_t\mid x,\widetilde o_{<t})\right].
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbb E_{\mathrm{correct}}$ averages over the correct responses
and $\mathbb E_{\mathrm{all}}$ over all of them.
Averaging within each segment prevents the longer reasoning sequences
from dominating the loss by token count. The weight $\beta$ balances
confidence supervision against reasoning supervision, and we set
$\beta=0.5$ so that the two terms contribute equally.

\paragraph{Integration with confidence-aware RL.}
CalibSFT only changes the confidence prior and leaves the RL objective
unchanged, so it can be applied before any confidence-aware RL method.
We evaluate it across RL methods in Section~\ref{sec:overall_results}
and analyze its effect on confidence concentration after RL in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration_analysis}.
We further examine its generality across confidence formats and base
models in Section~\ref{sec:generality}.
Finally, we ablate the confidence target construction, the mixing
weight $\lambda$, and the correctness-conditional supervision in
Appendix~\ref{sec:ablation_studies}.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Baselines.} 

We compare methods spanning answer-only and confidence-aware training. \textbf{Base}
denotes the checkpoint without task-specific training, while \textbf{RLVR} optimizes only
answer correctness. We use confidence-aware RL baselines including
\textbf{RLCR}~\citep{damani2026beyond}, \textbf{ReDoubt}~\citep{bani2026rewarding},
\textbf{CoCA}~\citep{li2026confidence}, \textbf{DCPO}~\citep{ma2026decoupling}, and
\textbf{C3RL}~\citep{yang2026scaling}. To measure the effect of \textbf{CalibSFT}, we
evaluate each confidence-aware RL method from both the Base and CalibSFT
checkpoints. All methods use Qwen3-8B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} as the
default model and follow the \textbf{DAPO} training recipe~\citep{yu2025dapo}.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use \textbf{DeepScaleR-Preview}~\citep{deepscaler2025} as the mathematical training dataset.
We evaluate on a broad range of in-distribution (ID) and out-of-domain (OOD)
datasets spanning different levels of difficulty.
For ID mathematical reasoning, we use \textbf{DeepScaleR-Eval},
\textbf{MATH-500}~\citep{hendrycks2021math}, \textbf{MinervaMath}~\citep{lewkowycz2022minerva},
\textbf{OlympiadBench}~\citep{he2024olympiadbench}, \textbf{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, and
\textbf{AIME 2024--2026}~\citep{dekoninck2026matharena}.
For general reasoning, we use \textbf{HotpotQA}~\citep{yang2018hotpotqa},
\textbf{MuSiQue}~\citep{trivedi2022musique}, \textbf{TriviaQA}~\citep{joshi2017triviaqa},
\textbf{NQOpen}~\citep{lee2019latent}, \textbf{PopQA}~\citep{mallen2023trust},
\textbf{WebQuestions}~\citep{berant2013semantic}, \textbf{DROP}~\citep{dua2019drop}, and
\textbf{LiveBenchReasoning}~\citep{white2024livebench}.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
We evaluate model performance using complementary metrics that capture answer
correctness, confidence discrimination, and absolute calibration. We report \textbf{Pass@1} for answer
correctness, \textbf{AUROC} for ranking correct responses above incorrect responses, and
\textbf{Brier score}~\citep{brier1950verification} and
\textbf{ECE}~\citep{guo2017calibration} for probabilistic calibration.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
During evaluation, we use temperature 0.6 and sample 32 responses per problem
for the AIME benchmarks and 4 responses per problem for all other benchmarks. All experiments are conducted on a
single node with eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs.  We report our hyperparameter setting and training details in Appendix~\ref{sec:detailed_setup}.

\paragraph{CalibSFT consistently benefits diverse confidence-aware RL methods.}
 As shown in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}, initializing RL from CalibSFT
reduces Brier score and ECE and increases AUROC across all five confidence-aware RL
baselines on both mathematical and general-reasoning benchmarks.
As a concrete example, CalibSFT reduces DCPO's ECE from 44.59 to 20.25 while lifting its
AUROC from 64.19 to 74.73 on MinervaMath.
Moreover, these calibration gains come without sacrificing accuracy. The average Pass@1 improves by
1.19 points in-distribution and 1.49 points out-of-distribution across the five methods.
We provide detailed per-dataset results in Table~\ref{tab:appendix_id_results}-\ref{tab:appendix_ood_results} (Appendix~\ref{sec:per_dataset_results}).
Overall, these results establish CalibSFT as a plug-and-play initialization that consistently
strengthens confidence-aware RL for better calibration.


\paragraph{CalibSFT and RL contribute complementary gains.}
To disentangle their contributions, we compare CalibSFT and RLVR individually.
CalibSFT alone substantially mitigates overconfidence, with ECE dropping from 36.26 to 14.82 in-distribution and from 46.94 to 24.16 out-of-distribution.
In contrast, Base and RLVR remain severely miscalibrated, indicating that answer correctness alone is insufficient to reshape confidence.
However, calibration does not imply better confidence ranking: CalibSFT's OOD AUROC even declines slightly from 65.71 to 61.38, reflecting the limited generalization of supervised training. 
Confidence-aware RL fills exactly this gap by directly optimizing discrimination, and combining it with CalibSFT yields the strongest overall performance.

\subsection{Calibration Analysis}
\label{sec:calibration_analysis}

\paragraph{CalibSFT aligns confidence with accuracy across tasks.}
A well-calibrated model should express higher confidence on tasks it can reliably solve and lower confidence on harder ones. 
To test this, we compute Pearson and Spearman correlations between per-dataset mean confidence and per-dataset accuracy for each RL method on the ID and OOD splits separately.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:confidence_correlation}, CalibSFT improves Spearman correlation for all RL baselines on both mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks. 
For example, DCPO's Spearman correlation rises from 0.619 to 0.976 on mathematical tasks and from 0.548 to 0.643 on general reasoning.
Pearson correlation also improves consistently across all baselines and splits (e.g., from 0.505 to 0.729 for RLCR on OOD).
These results indicate that CalibSFT helps the model assign higher confidence to tasks it is more likely to answer correctly.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Confidence estimation for LLMs.}
Confidence estimates for a model answer can be derived from many signals, of which four
families cover most existing methods and differ in what they require at inference
time~\citep{geng2024survey,liu2025uncertainty}. The most direct signal is the probability
the model assigns to its own answer tokens, used as the confidence either as it is or
after a layer fitted on labeled data corrects
it~\citep{kadavath2022language,liu2024litcab}. Probing methods instead train a classifier
on the hidden states or on the layer-wise trajectory that produces the
answer~\citep{azaria2023internal,eusebi2026reading,srey2026signals}, which requires access
to activations rather than logits. When a model exposes neither, sampling-based methods
recover confidence from behavior alone and measure how far several sampled answers
agree~\citep{kuhn2023semantic,del2026uncertainty,taubenfeld2025confidence}, at the cost of
repeated generations per question. Verbalized confidence removes that cost as well: the
model states a numerical or linguistic score alongside its answer within a single
generation~\citep{lin2022teaching,tian2023just,xiong2024can,yang2024verbalized}. We study
the verbalized setting for reasoning models, where the score follows a reasoning trace and
the final answer. Such a score is informative only once it matches accuracy: the values an
untrained model reports concentrate on a few points of the
scale~\citep{dai2026rescalingconfidence,cacioli2026saturation} and rank its errors worse
than its own token probabilities~\citep{ni2024honest,tao2025revisiting}.

\paragraph{Confidence calibration for LLMs.}
Calibration requires the reported confidence to match the empirical probability that the
answer is correct, and a method either rescales a score after the model has produced it or
changes the model that produces it. Rescaling is the standard treatment for
probability-based signals: a temperature or a bias layer is fitted on a labeled held-out
set~\citep{guo2017calibration,liu2024litcab}, and label-free variants align the score with
a reference model or project the two predictive distributions onto a calibrated
one~\citep{luo2026your,kong2026calibration}. Probes need no separate step, since they are
classifiers already trained on correctness
labels~\citep{azaria2023internal,srey2026signals}, and a sampling-based estimate is
calibrated by choosing how agreement among samples maps to a
probability~\citep{kuhn2023semantic,del2026uncertainty}. A verbalized score can be
rescaled the same way, but only through extra queries per question or an extra pass that
rewrites the reported value~\citep{wang2026calibrating,yeh2026retrieval}, and neither
changes how the model generates the score in the first place.

Post-training instead changes the model that emits the score, and it targets the
verbalized score because that score needs no logit access and no extra samples at
deployment. Supervised fine-tuning supervises the score with correctness signals collected
from the model's own
responses~\citep{lin2022teaching,zhang2024rtuning,jang2025verbalized,liu2024uncertainty,xu2024sayself,hager2025uncertainty,seo2025advice,zhang2026lovec,li2025conftuner}.
Reinforcement learning adds a calibration term to the reward of RLVR, which by itself
optimizes answer correctness and leaves the confidence
unsupervised~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}; the term is a proper scoring rule on
the reported confidence, and the methods differ in where the score is placed, what it is
trained toward, and how credit reaches its
tokens~\citep{leng2025taming,damani2026beyond,bani2026rewarding,tan2026effectiveness,li2026confidence,finlay2026caliber,shi2026sage,yu2026semantic,li2026orce,ma2026decoupling,wang2026process,yang2026scaling,zhang2026collaboration}.
These objectives learn from the confidence values the base model already samples, which
Section~\ref{sec:motivation} shows are concentrated on a few high values and stay
concentrated while the calibration reward is optimized. In this paper, we analyze this
prior and propose CalibSFT, an SFT stage that initializes verbalized confidence before
confidence-aware RL.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_saturation}}
\label{sec:confidence_saturation_proof}
\begin{proof}
We fix $x$ and the expected rewards $u_i$, and differentiate with respect
to the confidence logits.
The softmax derivative is
$\partial\pi_j/\partial\eta_i=\pi_j(\mathbf 1\{j=i\}-\pi_i)$.
It follows that
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial J_x}{\partial\eta_i}
    &=\sum_j u_j\pi_j(\mathbf 1\{j=i\}-\pi_i) \\
    &=\pi_i(u_i-J_x).
\end{align}
Since $J_x$ is a convex combination of the $u_i$ values,
$|u_i-J_x|\leq\Delta_x$, which gives the coordinate-wise bound.
If $\pi_k=1-\varepsilon$, then
\begin{equation}
    |u_k-J_x|
    =\left|\sum_{i\ne k}\pi_i(u_k-u_i)\right|
    \leq\varepsilon\Delta_x.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{align}
    \|\nabla_\eta J_x\|_1
    &=\pi_k|u_k-J_x|+\sum_{i\ne k}\pi_i|u_i-J_x| \\
    &\leq(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon\Delta_x
      +\varepsilon\Delta_x \\
    &\leq 2\varepsilon\Delta_x.
\end{align}
\end{proof}
The gradient is the expectation of the likelihood-ratio estimator
$U\nabla_\eta\log\pi_I$, where $I\sim\pi$ and
$U=-(c_I-Z)^2$, with $Z$ drawn from its fixed conditional distribution
given $x$, independently of $I$.
This matches RLCR's reward $Z-(c_I-Z)^2$ (Preliminaries) up to the
term $Z$, which does not depend on $c_I$. Assuming $C\perp Z\mid x$
as in Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_support}, this term
contributes an $\eta$-independent additive constant to $J_x$ that
does not affect this gradient.
A baseline depending only on $x$ leaves this expectation unchanged.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_support}}
\label{sec:confidence_support_proof}
\begin{proof}
Conditional independence gives
$\mathbb E[Z-p_x\mid x,C]=0$. Expanding the square therefore yields
\begin{align}
    \mathbb E[(C-Z)^2\mid x]
    &=\mathbb E[(Z-p_x)^2\mid x]
      +\mathbb E[(C-p_x)^2\mid x] \\
    &=p_x(1-p_x)+\mathbb E[(C-p_x)^2\mid x].
\end{align}
For any $\delta>0$, the second term satisfies
\begin{align}
    \mathbb E[(C-Z)^2\mid x]-p_x(1-p_x)
    &=\mathbb E[(C-p_x)^2\mid x] \\
    &\geq\delta^2\Pr(|C-p_x|\geq\delta\mid x) \\
    &=\delta^2(1-m_\delta(x)).
\end{align}
Thus, assigning little probability mass near $p_x$ incurs excess Brier
loss even when confidence values in that neighborhood remain possible.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Restricted confidence support.}
As a special case, suppose $\Pr(C\in S_x\mid x)=1$ for a nonempty
set $S_x\subseteq[0,1]$, and define
$d_x=\operatorname{dist}(p_x,S_x)=\inf_{c\in S_x}|c-p_x|$.
If $d_x>0$, then $m_{d_x}(x)=0$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_support} with $\delta=d_x$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[(C-Z)^2\mid x]
    \geq p_x(1-p_x)+\operatorname{dist}(p_x,S_x)^2.
\end{equation}
For $d_x=0$, the same bound follows directly from the decomposition above.
If $S_x$ is closed, a predictor that always outputs a nearest point in
$S_x$ attains the bound.
Confidence values are effectively drawn from a small discrete set
throughout this paper (Figure~\ref{fig:initial_confidence_prior}), so
$\operatorname{dist}(p_x,S_x)$ measures how far the nearest available
value falls from $p_x$, translating the observed concentration
directly into excess Brier risk.

\paragraph{Best-of-$G$ sampling under a concentrated policy.}
Given the policy and a question $x$, draw $G$ independent
confidence samples $C_1,\ldots,C_G$ from its conditional distribution.
Each sample lies outside $\{c:|c-p_x|<\delta\}$ with probability
$1-m_\delta(x)$, and by independence all $G$ samples do so with
probability $(1-m_\delta(x))^G$. On that event, even the sample closest
to $p_x$ has squared error at least $\delta^2$, so
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E\!\left[\min_{i\leq G}(C_i-p_x)^2\mid x\right]
    \geq\delta^2(1-m_\delta(x))^G.
\end{equation}
With the group size $G=8$ used in our RL runs, a policy that rarely
samples near $p_x$ is unlikely to produce a well-calibrated confidence
anywhere in the group.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_target_moments}}
\label{sec:confidence_target_proof}
We analyze the unrounded targets under the original sampling distribution,
before response filtering and target balancing.
The random variables $Q_x$ and $C^{\star}_i$ correspond to the observed
$q_x$ and $c^{\star}_i$ in Equation~\ref{eq:calibsft_target}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_target_moments}]
Fix $x$ and write $v_x=p_x(1-p_x)$.
Conditional independence gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[Q_x\mid x]=\mathbb E[Z_i\mid x]=p_x,
    \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q_x\mid x)=\frac{v_x}{n}.
\end{equation}
Since $Z_i$ is included in $Q_x$, their conditional covariance is
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cov}(Q_x,Z_i\mid x)=\frac{v_x}{n}.
\end{equation}
Linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb E[C^{\star}_i\mid x]=p_x$.
Expanding the variance yields
\begin{align}
    \operatorname{Var}(C^{\star}_i\mid x)
    &=\lambda^2\frac{v_x}{n}+(1-\lambda)^2v_x
      +2\lambda(1-\lambda)\frac{v_x}{n} \\
    &=v_x\left[\frac{1}{n}
      +(1-\lambda)^2\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\right].
\end{align}
Now fix $a$ at which $\ell$ is finite. Since $\ell(a,t)$ is affine in $t$,
unbiasedness gives
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[\ell(a,C^{\star}_i)\mid x]
    =p_x\ell(a,1)+(1-p_x)\ell(a,0)
    =\mathbb E[\ell(a,Z_i)\mid x].
\end{equation}
Because $\ell$ is strictly proper, this expected loss is uniquely
minimized at $a=p_x$. Thus the mixture, the group average, and an
individual correctness label induce the same expected scoring loss for a
prediction based on $x$.
For the Brier loss $\ell(a,z)=(a-z)^2$, the extension is
$\ell(a,t)=(a-t)^2+t(1-t)$, so the squared loss against a soft target
has the same minimizer. The same argument applies to the binary
logarithmic loss~\citep{gneiting2007strictly}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Effect of the response count and mixing weight.}
At $\lambda=0$, the target is the binary label with variance $v_x$;
at $\lambda=1$, it is the group average with variance $v_x/n$.
For a fixed $\lambda<1$, the variance approaches $(1-\lambda)^2v_x$ as
$n$ increases, reflecting the retained contribution of the individual
outcome.
Within any observed group,
$C^{\star}_i-C^{\star}_j=(1-\lambda)(Z_i-Z_j)$, so the targets of a
correct and an incorrect response differ by $1-\lambda$.

\paragraph{Conditioning on the generated response.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:confidence_target_moments} concerns the success probability $p_x$ before observing
a particular response. To make the effect of conditioning explicit, let
$h_i$ include $x$ and information from response $i$, and define
$p_{h_i}=\Pr(Z_i=1\mid h_i)$.
If the other responses are independent of $h_i$ conditional on $x$, then
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb E[C^{\star}_i\mid h_i]
    =\left(1-\lambda+\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)p_{h_i}
     +\lambda\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)p_x.
\end{equation}
The target thus shrinks the response-conditional probability toward the
question's success probability. These results justify the mean and
variance of this supervision signal under the original sampling distribution;
it does not establish response-conditional calibration after target balancing
and token-level SFT. Those effects are evaluated empirically.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:detailed_setup}

\subsection{Training Pipelines}

All runs use Qwen3-8B as the base model. From DeepScaleR-Preview we remove
duplicate questions and questions whose gold answers disagree across
duplicates, which leaves 38{,}915 questions. We randomly hold out 2{,}000 of
them as a test split (DeepScaleR-Eval) and use the remaining 36{,}915 as the
training split (DeepScaleR-Train), which is used to construct the SFT data for
CalibSFT and the downstream RL training data.

\paragraph{SFT data construction.}
\label{sec:calibsft_data_details}
We sample $n=50$ responses per question from DeepScaleR-Train with the base
model, using temperature 1.0, top-$p$ 0.95, and a cap of 8{,}192 tokens, which
gives 1{,}845{,}750 responses in total. Each question's success rate $q_x$ is
computed from all 50 responses before any filtering. We then discard responses
that do not follow the required
\texttt{<think>}/\texttt{<answer>}/\texttt{<confidence>} format and responses
that hit the token cap without terminating, which are typically repetitive or
looping generations. This removes 401{,}634 responses and retains
1{,}444{,}116, with $q_x$ kept fixed. For each retained response we form the
target $c^{\star}_i=\lambda q_x+(1-\lambda)z_i$ with $\lambda=0.5$ and replace
the confidence suffix with it, preserving the sampled reasoning and answer. In
the main probability format, the target is rendered to two decimal places in
the trailing \texttt{<confidence>} tag, which yields 100 distinct target
values. For balanced sampling, we group examples by the numeric target rounded
to six decimal places and sample the same number from every nonempty group,
using the smallest group size as the default count. We apply this rule either
through a per-epoch sampler or when constructing an offline balanced split; the
offline split used in our main experiments contains 349 examples per target
value, that is, 34{,}900 examples drawn from 11{,}539 distinct questions.

\paragraph{CalibSFT.}
We train on the balanced split with the correctness-conditional loss mask and
$\beta=0.5$: correct responses supervise both the reasoning/answer and the
confidence segments, whereas incorrect responses supervise only the confidence
segment. We use AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$) with no weight decay and
gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, a constant learning rate of $2\times10^{-6}$
after 10 warm-up steps, and a global batch size of 256 sequences truncated to
16{,}384 tokens. We train for 180 optimization steps, roughly 1.3 epochs over
the balanced split, on eight A100 GPUs.

\paragraph{Confidence-aware RL.}
For downstream RL, each prompt group contains 8 sampled responses, with each
response capped at 8,192 tokens. We train with batches of 64 prompt groups for
160 optimization steps. For methods using DAPO dynamic sampling, this
corresponds to approximately 600 generation batches. Dynamic sampling retains only groups
with mixed correctness outcomes and discards groups whose responses are all
correct or all incorrect. To accelerate training for these methods, we
remove questions for which all sampled responses from the base model are
correct before RL training begins. Each method retains its own reward and
credit assignment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct RL baselines.} RLVR, RLCR~\citep{damani2026beyond}, CoCA~\citep{li2026confidence}, and DCPO~\citep{ma2026decoupling} are trained
    directly from the Qwen3-8B base checkpoint. They share the DAPO training
    recipe but retain their respective correctness, calibration-target, and
    credit-assignment objectives.
    \item \textbf{ReDoubt.} ReDoubt is initialized from the
    trained RLVR checkpoint and then optimized with its clipped log-score
    confidence objective~\citep{bani2026rewarding}. When incorporated with CalibSFT, we first train CalibSFT on the RLVR checkpoint and then continue with ReDoubt RL.
    \item \textbf{C3RL.} C3RL~\citep{yang2026scaling} combines correctness,
    calibration, and reference-accuracy rewards. C3RL uses the
    digit confidence format, expressing confidence as an integer from 0 to 9
    and mapping it to $[0,1]$ by dividing by 9. The CalibSFT initialization
    for C3RL is also trained in this format. We use the base model's
    success rates over sampled responses to label each training question as all correct,
    partially correct, or all incorrect. The reference reward encourages
    correct answers to previously all-incorrect questions and penalizes
    incorrect answers to previously all-correct questions. To retain these
    questions and their reference-reward signals, we disable DAPO dynamic
    sampling and all-correct filtering, using the full RL training split
    for C3RL both with and without CalibSFT initialization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
For the main results, we use temperature $0.6$ and sample four responses per
question on non-AIME mathematical and OOD benchmarks and 32 responses per
question on AIME. Pass@1 is the mean correctness over these sampled responses.
The supervision and target ablations retain their recorded evaluation settings.
Confidence is parsed from the final probability field and normalized to $[0,1]$.
We use 10 equal-width bins for calculating ECE. Unless otherwise noted, RL results use
the final checkpoint after 160 optimization steps, while SFT-only results use
the checkpoint at the end of their respective SFT runs.

\paragraph{Answer verification.}
Every benchmark is scored by a deterministic verifier except TriviaQA. On the
mathematical benchmarks and HotpotQA, a response is correct if its extracted
answer is symbolically equivalent to the gold answer under
\texttt{math\_verify}, or if the two agree after lowercasing, removing
punctuation and articles, and collapsing whitespace. NQOpen and MuSiQue apply
the same normalized match against any gold alias. PopQA follows its official
protocol and accepts a response that contains a gold alias, and WebQuestions
requires the set of predicted answer items to match the gold set exactly. DROP
uses the official DROP normalization and counts a response correct only on an
exact match with one of its references. LiveBenchReasoning uses LiveBench's own
deterministic graders for its spatial, web-of-lies, and zebra-puzzle tasks.
TriviaQA admits many surface forms per answer, so we score it with an LLM
judge: Qwen3-8B receives the question, the reference answer, and the candidate
answer, and returns a correct or incorrect verdict. The same
symbolic-equivalence-or-normalized-match rule produces the correctness labels
$z_i$ that build the CalibSFT targets and the correctness rewards during RL.

The construction of confidence targets in CalibSFT is inspired by proper scoring rules, which serve as a foundation for calibration.
To verify the effectiveness of this target design, we compare CalibSFT
with targets based on group-level correctness (CSFT)~\citep{jang2025verbalized},
token probabilities (UaIT)~\citep{liu2024uncertainty}, semantic consistency
(SaySelf)~\citep{xu2024sayself}, and rubric-based LLM judgments
(LoVeC)~\citep{zhang2026lovec}, using a shared SFT training set and the same
optimization objective.



For a fair comparison, we construct the training data from a shared response
pool sampled uniformly across empirical success rates so that the comparison
covers both easier and harder questions. For each question in
DeepScaleR-Train, we use 10 responses generated by Qwen3-8B, with $z_i\in\{0,1\}$
denoting correctness and $q_x=\frac{1}{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}z_i$ the empirical
success rate. We select 300 questions at each $q_x\in\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.9\}$
and retain one correct and one incorrect response per question, yielding
5{,}400 responses from 2{,}700 questions with balanced correctness at each
success rate. SaySelf uses only its correct responses following its SFT setup.
On this shared subset, we construct the five confidence targets as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CSFT.} Both responses receive the group success rate, $c^{\star}_i=q_x$.

    \item \textbf{UaIT.} $c^{\star}_i=\exp(-\mathrm{NLL}_i)$, where $\mathrm{NLL}_i$ is the mean negative log-likelihood of the sampled response tokens under the generating model.

    \item \textbf{SaySelf.} We embed the ten responses to each question with Qwen3-Embedding-8B and cluster them at a cosine similarity threshold of 0.98. Each retained correct response is assigned the frequency of its cluster, $c^{\star}_i=|\mathcal M_i|/10$, where $\mathcal M_i$ is the cluster containing response $i$.

    \item \textbf{LoVeC.} An LLM judge scores each response on a 0--10 rubric (10: correct with sound reasoning; 8--9: correct with minor gaps; 5--7: partially correct or ambiguous; 2--4: meaningful but incorrect; 0--1: invalid or clearly wrong), normalized as $c^{\star}_i=s_i/10$.

    \item \textbf{CalibSFT.} Following our main design, the target combines group success rate with response correctness, $c^{\star}_i=0.5q_x+0.5z_i$.
\end{itemize}

All variants initialize from Qwen3-8B and use the same supervision objective:
reasoning and answer tokens are supervised on correct responses, while
confidence tokens are supervised on all responses. We train for 80 steps
(40 for SaySelf) with a batch size of 256 and a learning rate of
$2\times10^{-6}$. Table~\ref{tab:training_target_distribution} reports the
proportion of training targets in each of ten equal-width confidence
intervals across the five methods.

\paragraph{Target mixing weight.}
We construct confidence targets as
$c^{\star}_i=\lambda q_x+(1-\lambda)z_i$, where $q_x$ is the group
success rate and $z_i$ is the correctness of an individual response.
To evaluate the effect of the mixing weight, we compare
$\lambda\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ using the same subset as above
and keeping the SFT settings fixed.
At $\lambda=0$, targets are restricted to 0 and 1; this setting yields
the highest Brier score in Table~\ref{tab:lambda_ablation}.
At $\lambda=1$, correct and incorrect responses to the same question
receive identical targets, providing no within-question discrimination
signal; post-SFT AUROC is also lower than at $\lambda=0.5$.
Equal weighting achieves the highest AUROC and lowest Brier score on ID
benchmarks among the tested settings, so we use $\lambda=0.5$ as the default.

\paragraph{Correctness-conditional supervision.}
To verify the effectiveness of CalibSFT design, we ablate three choices in
the SFT loss: restricting reasoning and answer supervision to correct
responses, supervising confidence on all responses, and balancing the
segment losses. Table~\ref{tab:supervision_ablation} reports the results.
Confidence-only supervision leaves Pass@1 far behind the other variants,
showing that reasoning and answer supervision is necessary for accuracy.
Without segment balancing, calibration is markedly worse: Full-CE trails
Full-Balanced on both AUROC and ECE. Restricting
reasoning and answer supervision to correct responses slightly raises
accuracy, consistent with incorrect reasoning pulling the model toward
incorrect solutions, but restricting confidence supervision to correct
responses as well narrows its coverage and weakens calibration.
Supervising confidence on all responses while keeping reasoning and
answer supervision correct-only recovers this coverage: CalibSFT
achieves the best AUROC, Brier score, and ECE among all variants, with
Pass@1 close to the best. This combination best balances accuracy,
calibration, and confidence diversity, giving a well-calibrated starting point for downstream
confidence-aware RL.
